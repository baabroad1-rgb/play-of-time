How different kinds of users perceive their work with a computer

Alexander Belyshkin. First published in Vestnik Moskovskogo Universiteta, Series 14: Psychology, 2000, No. 3, pp. 82–93. AI-translated from Russian; the Russian original is here as a PDF and on usability.ru. · Download the translation as PDF

The last decade has seen information technology spread fast into the most varied areas of human activity. Ten years ago computers were used for narrow, specialised tasks; today they are a standard part of the workplace in mass occupations. As the computerisation of work goes on, it becomes ever more pressing to build effective and reliable means of human–computer dialogue that suit users of different training and skill. Cognitive ergonomics and psychology have produced a number of approaches to different sides of this problem (Burmistrov, 1993; Hacker, 1987; Roe, 1988).

Engineering psychology traditionally set out to make people compatible with the demands of new technical means, so many studies dealt with displays and controls, the layout of computerised workplaces, and regimes of work and rest at the computer (Salvendy, 1991). Analysis of what is specific to computerised work led to more psychologically oriented approaches. These include, for instance, studies of how people perceive information on displays and how other cognitive processes run at the computer (see, e.g., Lantsburg and Rosenblum, 1988; Hacker, 1987). Among the more interesting approaches that look at computerised work as a whole are studies of how users adapt psychologically to new information technology. They analyse how computerisation affects users’ personality and mental health, taking individual differences into account (Korunka, 1995; Johansson, 1984). There are also approaches to the cognitive-ergonomic side of introducing information technology. They work both at the level of general strategies for building computer systems that are easier for people to use (Hacker, 1987; Johansson, 1984) and at the level of designing particular software interfaces (Burmistrov, 1993; Roe, 1988). These works stress the need for psychologically grounded support of the user’s work, bringing the virtual world closer to how people naturally behave in ordinary surroundings.

For all their differences, these approaches have one thing in common: each needs to analyse the user’s subjective attitude to, or “view” of, their own work at the computer. The “image” of the work situation is central to the methodology of modern work psychology (Klimov, 1995; Leonova and Chernysheva, 1995), yet concrete studies of computerised work hardly touch on it. Our study attempted an empirical analysis of how subjective perceptions of computer work differ between kinds of users.

The main aim was to identify and analyse differences in how different groups of users represent their work with a computer, so that the findings could later serve for recommendations on effective support of computerised work. Studies of this kind are fairly new and have no established methods, so we prepared a special questionnaire to bring out the subjective characteristics by which users can rate their attitude to different sides of computer work. We also had to establish grounds for dividing users into groups worth comparing within this exploratory study.

The study had four tasks:

  • to develop a questionnaire for preliminary screening of potentially important features of users’ subjective perceptions of different sides of computer work;
  • to survey a broad range of users with this questionnaire;
  • to find, from the data, criteria for dividing the sample into groups that might differ in their perceptions of computer work;
  • to analyse the differences between these groups and draft preliminary recommendations for improving working conditions for different kinds of users.

Method

We developed a questionnaire to bring out different groups of factors that may shape subjective perceptions of computer work. They included subjective ratings of objective working conditions and of the tasks, as well as the user’s personal “view” of various work situations and their typical ways of behaving in them.

The questionnaire was structured: a long list of questions, each with a deliberately redundant set of possible answers. The respondent rated agreement with each answer on a four-point scale, usually “not at all – perhaps – true – quite true” or “almost never – sometimes – often – almost always”.

In all there were 21 questions with 294 answer items, 4 to 10 answers per question.1 The questions fell into nine blocks that, in our view, reflect important factors in how different groups of users form a subjective “image” of computer work:

  1. Personal data: sex, age, education, years of computer use.
  2. Self-rated computer skill: special knowledge and skills, competence, mastery compared with other users.
  3. Motives and goals: why and for what the user works with a computer (for instance, pragmatic motives such as income, convenience or prestige; direct job necessity; the computer as an object or as a tool of professional work; personal interest).
  4. Personification of the computer: whether the user gives the computer qualities of a living being or a partner, and what emotions they feel towards it.
  5. Workload: how much time the user spends at the computer, depending on working conditions and task difficulty.
  6. Causes of fatigue and ways of coping: how tiring computer work is for the user, what they see as the main causes of fatigue and how they usually try to overcome it.
  7. Attitude to computer failures: what the user sees as typical causes of faults and what strategies they use to fix them.

Two more blocks were included as separate instruments:

  1. Part of the Job Diagnostic Survey for the main subjective characteristics of work: variety, identity, significance, autonomy and feedback of the tasks performed (Hackman and Oldham, 1975).
  2. The Level of Subjective Control questionnaire (Bazhin, Golykina and Etkind, 1993).

Sample

Forty-six users took part: 25 women and 21 men aged 18 to 65, with a mean age of 31. They were employees of a company selling computer systems, editorial staff of a computer magazine, students of Moscow universities, and professional psychologists. Experience with computers averaged 7 years and ranged from 1 to 25 years.

Procedure

Each respondent filled in the questionnaire individually, with no time limit. It took about 30 minutes on average.

Analysis

First, the answers in each block were factorised to obtain summary scores, using principal components with VARIMAX rotation. Blocks taken from existing instruments (see above) were not factorised again; for them we used the standard scores proposed by their authors.

Second, we correlated the summary scores with users’ individual characteristics to find criteria for dividing the sample into groups.

Third, we ran a one-way analysis of variance on each summary score for each set of groups. This showed the most important differences in how different groups of users perceive computer work.

Results

Factor analysis

Factorisation greatly reduced the number of dependent variables, the raw ratings of each item. For each block we obtained a set of summary scores reflecting the “weightiest” components of the original answers. The answer matrix of 294 items was reduced to 75 scores and prepared for correlation analysis.

Correlation analysis

At this stage we tested correlations between the 75 summary scores and several independent variables that could, a priori, serve to divide users into groups: age, years of computer use, self-rated computer skill, and personality traits.

Age showed only 3 significant correlations with the summary scores, so it had little effect on the subjective ratings across the data. This was probably because the sample was fairly homogeneous in age. Age was therefore left out of further analysis.

The most significant correlations (p < 0.05 or better) were shown by years of computer use and self-rated skill: 12 and 18 respectively. These two variables were also strongly correlated with each other (r = 0.433; p < 0.005), so we combined them into one independent variable, which we called the user’s “subjective competence”. From the combination of experience and self-rated skill it was given three levels: low, medium and high. Accordingly the sample was divided into three groups, novices, intermediate users and experts, and these were compared in the further analysis.

The personality trait we used, the level of subjective control, or “internality”, also showed a fair number of stable correlations with the summary scores: 10 significant correlations with different indices of the questionnaire. We therefore also compared “externals” and “internals”. With our limited data, we divided respondents only by the index of general internality.

Almost all empirical studies treat sex as one of the most important factors in how people work with computers (see, e.g., Korunka, 1995; Johansson, 1984). So we also compared men and women.

Discussion

Novices, intermediate users and experts

Comparing users by subjective competence gave significant differences in four blocks of scores.

The block “motives and goals” brings together the dominant motives, aims and tasks of computer work. The higher the subjective competence, the more computer use is tied to pragmatic motives and direct job necessity. As for tasks, more “competent” users not only rate the computer higher as a necessary tool, they also see it as an object of work in its own right. This may be because as users master the computer, they are drawn more and more into a “computerised” way of thinking about the task. In other words, growing competence changes how users understand their tasks, which they increasingly frame in terms of information technology.

Significant differences were found in the block on personification of the computer. For seeing the computer as “alive” the relation is not monotonic: it is strongest in experts and novices and much weaker in intermediate users. One may suppose that personification does some work in the user’s activity. If personification lets the user “share” responsibility for a task with a “work partner” (Dormann, 1994), then experts use this in a positive way. Unlike novices, for them personification is purposeful: from long experience they know that the computer is a “thinking machine” and must be treated accordingly to start a full dialogue. For novices personification is a spontaneous reaction to frequent failures and the computer’s unpredictable “behaviour”, and it comes with a feeling of being inferior to the computer. Intermediate users personify less, perhaps because they are no longer naive enough to fear possible faults and difficulties, but not yet experienced enough to develop special ways of delegating responsibility.

Significant differences appear in the block on fatigue. They show that the groups weigh its causes differently. Experts, more than the others, name poor workplace ergonomics as the main cause, while for novices poor social support matters more. Novices may not yet have a clear image of their workplace, of the interface and of what the hardware and software can do, so shortcomings in how the work is equipped are masked by organisational and social problems. Experts usually need less outside help: they can find the source of a difficulty and a suitable way to solve it on their own.

Several significant differences were found in the block on the likely causes of faults and the typical strategies for fixing them. Experts tend to blame faults on the software and, when something fails, first check the operating system (for example, always restart the computer first). Novices usually believe they themselves caused the failure, and their most typical response is to ask for help. This partly echoes the results on personification and indirectly supports the idea of sharing responsibility as equals. Experienced users delegate responsibility for the technical side of the work to the computer. This relieves the subjective strain of the work and makes the situation more comfortable for themselves and for others.

Men and women

Comparing men and women gave significant differences in three blocks of scores, but far fewer than in the previous case.

In the block “motives and goals” the only significant difference was in “the computer as the object of work”, rated much higher by men than by women. This probably reflects the unsurprising fact that women are less inclined than men to deal with technical matters as such: installing hardware and software, fixing faults, maintenance and so on. In this sense women can be called “users” in the literal meaning of the word.

In the block “causes of fatigue”, men rate the harm of poor workplace ergonomics much higher, while for women poor social support of the work stands out more. This may also reflect different goals of work in the two groups: in our sample, women more often combined computer work with other professional duties.

In the block “causes of faults and strategies for fixing them” there were no notable differences in how men and women rate the causes of failure, nor in any preference for delegating responsibility (compare the absence of significant differences in personification). But there are significant differences in typical strategies. Women, more than men, tend to ask for help when something goes wrong. They also rate higher such a “passive” strategy as switching the computer off and resting. These results support the finding above that women are less ready to solve technical problems at the computer on their own.

Internals and externals

The level of subjective control did affect the scores, but only to a very limited extent. Just two scores differed significantly between externals and internals. The first, in the block “causes of fatigue”, showed that internals more often explain fatigue by poor social support. Since internals tend to rely on their own resources, this is somewhat unexpected. Perhaps internals read this item as also covering interruptions, outside distractions and other signs of poorly organised work. Notably, internals did not differ from externals in how often they ask for outside help. The second score shows that externals use a wider variety of strategies for dealing with faults: they more often describe their actions as falling outside the options offered in our questionnaire.

The very limited effect of this important personality trait may come from dividing the sample too coarsely, by the index of general internality alone. In further work it would be worth classifying respondents by other indices of the questionnaire as well, in particular internality in work relations.

Conclusion

The study showed that it is possible in principle to derive summary scores that describe how different groups of users perceive their work with a computer. The most important characteristics turned out to be the motives and goals of the work, the user’s special attitude to the computer as a partner in joint work (personification), the causes of fatigue and ways of coping with it, and the type of response to failures and difficulties at work. In these blocks we found systematic differences between groups of users who differed in subjective competence, sex and level of subjective control.

Although the study was exploratory, its results allow some general recommendations on working conditions and on how computerised tasks are presented, to make work easier for different kinds of users.

First, to form adequate motives and goals, users should be helped to understand more fully that the computer is not only a tool but also an object of their work. In other words, from the very start users should clearly understand that the computer can be set up to fit their needs and tasks.

Users of all groups show a marked tendency to personify the computer. Personification happens whether or not existing information technology supports it (Van Mulken, André and Müller, 1998). As noted above, personification can be seen as a positive factor that reduces the negative effects of the strain and complexity of computerised work. Makers of software and hardware should therefore consider special elements that encourage personification of the computer, within reason, which could give interaction with the computer the form of work among colleagues (Dormann, 1994).

To make work easier for novices, it is worth using various means to set clear and concrete tasks, to build into the software elements that reduce self-doubt, and to add more ways of giving full feedback.

For groups affected by specific causes of fatigue, such as poor workplace ergonomics or poor social support, it makes sense to look at organisational measures and at special software that widens the range of ways to rest and relieve tension under stress at a computerised workplace. Such software could give “reminders” or “prompts” during work. It could also include elements of relaxation (compare, e.g., Leonova and Kuznetsova, 1993).

Further research could go towards more concrete requirements for software. This needs a fuller and broader study of subjective perceptions of computer work, taking into account professional and individual differences, and of the mechanisms and ways of overcoming the negative effects of mass computerisation of work. The personification of the computer also deserves special attention and further study of its mechanisms. This seems especially relevant now that virtual user assistants are being developed in the form of so-called agents and synthetic actors (Long, 1999; Iba and Gervasio, 1999).
